The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes a HELOC as an open-end line of credit that lets you borrow repeatedly against your home equity. Think of it as a credit card secured by your home.

  • Draw period. You can borrow up to your credit limit during this period. Pat's guide notes that payments during this period are typically interest-only on the amount you have used.
  • Repayment period. You can no longer borrow, and you repay the balance. The CFPB notes that payments are often much higher at this stage.
  • Interest. HELOCs usually have a variable rate, so payments may change from month to month.
  • Second position. A HELOC is a second mortgage that sits behind your first mortgage. You pay both.

Building takes time and the costs arrive in stages. Pat's guide notes that a HELOC can suit home improvement projects because you can borrow what you need as you go, instead of all at once. That draw-as-you-go idea is the reason people ask about HELOCs for an ADU.

It is also the place to be careful. Ask whether a line's limit and draw rules match your builder's payment schedule, especially for the first payments.

Your equity is your home's value minus what you owe on your mortgage. Lenders confirm value with a traditional appraisal or, in some cases, an automated valuation model. The lender sets the limit based on its own rules and your finances, so any figure you have in mind is a starting point, not an answer.   • Homeowners with meaningful equity who want to keep their current first mortgage as it is.
  • Homeowners who expect to pay a builder in stages and are comfortable with a payment that can change.
  • Homeowners who need a fixed, predictable payment from day one. A home equity loan has a set schedule, so compare it. See HELOC vs cash-out refinance.
  • Homeowners who would struggle if the payment rose after the draw period ends.
  • Your home is the collateral. The CFPB says that if you fall behind or cannot repay on schedule, you could lose your home.
  • The line can change. The CFPB notes a lender might freeze your ability to take out more money if your financial circumstances change, or if your home value drops significantly.
  • Payments can rise. Variable rates and the switch to the repayment period can both increase your payment.
  • There are costs. Pat's guide lists appraisal, origination, title or recording and other administrative fees. See costs to expect.
  • How long is the draw period, and what happens to my payment when it ends?
  • Is the rate variable, and can I switch part of the balance to a fixed rate?
  • Can you freeze or change my line, and under what conditions?
  • What does the line require for me to draw funds for construction payments?
  • What are all the fees? Can I see a Loan Estimate to compare lenders?
