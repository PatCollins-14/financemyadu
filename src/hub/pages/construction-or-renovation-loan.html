Pat helps homeowners finance renovations, including projects that add an ADU. This page explains how these loans work in general. [PAT: which renovation and construction programs you offer, and what you can say about each]

Fannie Mae's ADU page names two examples:

  • Renovation loan. For homeowners buying or refinancing a one-unit property who want to construct or install a new ADU. Fannie Mae calls its version HomeStyle Renovation.
  • Construction-to-permanent loan. For borrowers building a new one-unit home with an ADU, financing the home and the ADU construction together.

Fannie Mae also states that these ADU rules do not apply to every property. ADUs are not eligible with a 2-4 unit dwelling or when a manufactured home is the primary residence, and properties with multiple ADUs are not eligible for Fannie Mae financing.

Some programs can. Fannie Mae's ADU page describes a renovation loan for homeowners who buy or refinance a one-unit property and want to construct or install a new ADU. Whether other renovation work, such as a kitchen or bathroom, can be in the same loan depends on the program and on the lender. Ask before you plan the budget around it. With a HELOC or a cash-out refinance, the lender looks at your home and finances as they are today. With a renovation or construction loan, the project itself is part of the picture, so lenders commonly ask for information about the work. Ask your lender exactly what it needs, how funds are released as work progresses, and who checks that work is done. [PAT: how draws and inspections work for the programs you offer]

  • Homeowners who are buying or refinancing anyway and want to ask whether the ADU can be part of that one loan.
  • Homeowners building a new home with an ADU.
  • Homeowners who do not want to replace their current first mortgage. A renovation loan of this kind is described as part of a purchase or refinance, so ask how it would treat your existing loan. See keeping your first-mortgage rate.
  • Homeowners whose project does not meet the lender's property rules.

More structure can mean more paperwork and more steps before and during construction. It can also mean one loan instead of two. The trade-off is your time and flexibility against simplicity. Closing costs apply here too. See costs to expect.

  • How are funds released during construction?
  • What happens if costs change or the project is delayed?
