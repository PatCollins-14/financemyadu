Your options

Which Option Fits You?

There is no single ADU loan. These are the main ways homeowners pay for an ADU. The right one depends on your equity, your current mortgage, your age and your goal.

Your first mortgage stays in place.

HELOC or home equity loan

You borrow against your equity. A HELOC lets you draw money as you need it. A home equity loan gives you one lump sum.

Main trade-offIt is a second loan secured by your home. HELOC rates usually change over time, and you must repay what you borrow.
Ask a lenderHow is my limit set? What happens when the draw period ends? What are the closing costs?

Your first mortgage is replaced.

Cash-out refinance

A new, larger mortgage pays off your current one and you receive the difference in cash, with a new rate, term and payment.

Main trade-offIf you already have a low first-mortgage rate, replacing that loan may not be ideal. There are closing costs, and your balance resets higher.
Ask a lenderHow does the new loan compare with my current one over the time I plan to stay?

Built around the work being done.

Renovation or construction loan

Fannie Mae describes a renovation loan that can finance a new ADU when a homeowner buys or refinances a one-unit home.

Ask a lenderHow are funds released during construction? What plans and contractor details do you need?

You keep paying taxes, insurance and upkeep.

Reverse mortgage (HECM), homeowners 62+

You must pay property taxes, homeowners insurance and any HOA fees, keep the home in good condition, and live there as your primary residence. If you do not, the loan can become due.

Your project

Every Kind of ADU Raises Its Own Financing Questions

An ADU is a self-contained living space on the same lot as your main home. Whether it is attached, detached or inside your house, bring the type to your first conversation.

{{art_attached}}

Attached ADU

Connected to your home.

Fannie Mae counts an ADU attached to the main home as one kind of ADU. It still needs its own space for living, sleeping, cooking and bathrooms.

Ask:Does the lender treat an attached unit differently from a detached one?
{{art_detached}}

Detached ADU

A separate unit in your backyard.

Fannie Mae notes an ADU can be fully detached from the main home. It must be reachable without going through the main home and offer some privacy.

Ask:What does the lender need to see about the plans?
{{art_garage}}

Garage Conversion

Existing space, new use.

The structure already exists, so the work and the plans look different from new construction. Your city sets the permit rules.

Ask:Does the lender need permits and plans before it can fund?
{{art_above}}

Above-Garage ADU

Living space over a garage.

Fannie Mae gives a living area over a garage as an example of an attached ADU.

Ask:How is the finished value estimated for a unit like this?
{{art_two}}

Two-Story ADU

A taller unit on a smaller footprint.

A larger project means a larger amount to finance, so the plans and the budget matter even more.

Ask:Will the lender finance a project of this size?
{{art_jadu}}

JADU

A small unit inside your home.

The City of Pasadena describes a Junior ADU as capped at 500 square feet and built within the existing or proposed single-family home or attached non-habitable space.

Ask:How would a lender treat a unit inside the main home?

Sources: Fannie Mae · City of Pasadena (read 2026-10-23). Other cities have their own rules.

How Lenders
Look at an ADU

Some programs may consider projected rental income or the value of your property after the ADU is built. Some do not. The answer depends on the loan program, not on the ADU alone.

For example, Fannie Mae's published guide allows rental income from one existing ADU in certain purchase and limited cash-out refinance loans, and limits how much of your qualifying income it can be. An ADU that is not built yet is a different question to ask your lender.

Rental income is not guaranteed and may not count toward qualification under every program.

Investor loans that qualify a rental property on its rent, such as the DSCR loans described on Pat's Non-QM page, are a different situation from an owner-occupied home with an ADU. Do not assume they apply to your project. [PAT: confirm which programs you offer that consider ADU rental income or after-ADU value]

Source: Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-3.8-02, read 2026-10-23. Lenders set their own rules and may differ.

Homeowners 62 and older

Using a Reverse Mortgage to Help Fund an ADU

A Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM) is a reverse mortgage for homeowners 62 and older. Before you can get one, you must meet with a HUD-approved counselor so you understand the terms and obligations.

What you must keep doing

  • Pay your property taxes, homeowners insurance and any HOA fees on time.
  • Keep the home in good condition.
  • Live in the home as your primary residence.

If you do not meet these obligations, the loan can become due. A reverse mortgage balance grows over time as interest and fees are added, which reduces the equity left in your home.
