Will financing an ADU affect my current mortgage rate?

Last reviewed: October 23, 2026

Short answerIt depends on how you finance the ADU. A HELOC or home equity loan is a second loan, so your first mortgage and its rate stay in place. A cash-out refinance replaces your first mortgage with a new loan at a new rate. Some renovation loans are described as part of a purchase or refinance, and a reverse mortgage pays off an existing mortgage first. Ask any lender what happens to your current loan.

What happens to your first mortgage, by option

OptionYour current first mortgage
HELOC or home equity loanStays in place. These are second mortgages that you pay in addition to the first one, according to the CFPB.
Cash-out refinanceReplaced. Our guide says the new loan pays off your current mortgage and gives you the difference in cash, with a new rate, term and payment.
Renovation loan (as Fannie Mae describes it)Described for borrowers who purchase or refinance a one-unit property. Ask whether yours would be replaced. [PAT: how the renovation programs you offer treat the existing mortgage]
Reverse mortgage (62+)Paid off first. Our guide says reverse mortgage funds first pay off an existing mortgage, and any money left is available to use.

Why it matters

Your first mortgage is the largest loan you have. Its rate and payment shape your monthly budget for years. If its terms work well for you, replacing it deserves a hard look. Our refinance guide says that if you already have a low interest rate, refinancing your first mortgage may not be ideal, and a HELOC or home equity loan can be an alternative.

The reverse is also true. If your current mortgage is not working for you, a refinance may improve more than your ADU funding. The choice depends on your own numbers. Our guide says a refinance can make sense when the new loan improves cash flow, interest cost, loan term, payment stability or access to equity enough to justify the closing costs.

How to compare fairly

  1. Write down your current rate, term and balance. [PAT: whether to suggest clients bring their latest mortgage statement to the first call]
  2. Ask each lender how it treats that loan under each option.
  3. Compare the total cost of each path over the years you expect to keep the loan, using Loan Estimates.

Who this fits

  • Homeowners with a first mortgage they are happy with who are weighing a second loan.
  • Homeowners whose first mortgage no longer suits them and who are open to replacing it.

Who this does not fit

  • Homeowners who cannot look at their current loan terms. Without them, no honest comparison is possible.

Trade-offs

Keeping your first mortgage protects its terms, but a second loan adds another payment and its own costs. Replacing your first mortgage can simplify things into one loan, but you give up the old terms and pay closing costs. Neither is free of trade-offs.

What to ask a lender

  • Under this option, what happens to my current first mortgage?
  • What would my new total monthly obligations be under each option?
  • What are the total closing costs under each option?
  • If my current loan has features I value, would I lose them?
